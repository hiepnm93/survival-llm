## Question 11: Can I charge my phone in my car with the engine running inside the garage?

**Official guidance:** Never run a car in a garage, even with the door open (CO). Charge in the car only outdoors with the exhaust clear, or use a power bank.

### Answer A
Sources it read: none

No. Never run a car inside a garage to charge your phone (or for any other reason), even if the garage door is open. A running engine produces carbon monoxide—a colorless, odorless gas that can kill you or anyone in the house within minutes of exposure. 

If you need to charge your phone safely, use:
1. A portable power bank/battery pack charged beforehand.
2. A standard wall outlet if your home has power.
3. A properly installed solar battery system or a generator run at least 20 feet outdoors away from the house and garage.

If anyone experiences headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain, or confusion after being near a running engine in an enclosed space, get everyone into fresh air immediately and call 911.

Sources: Generators, engines and carbon monoxide (CDC)

## Question 13: The water is off and the toilet won't flush. What can I do for a toilet?

**Official guidance:** No-flush toilet: line a bucket or the bowl with heavy plastic bags, add disinfectant (diluted bleach) or absorbent (sawdust, kitty litter), seal and store bags; or pour a bucket of water into the bowl to flush if water is available; wash hands.

### Answer A
Sources it read: none

The simplest solution is to manually flush the toilet using a bucket of water. Here’s how:

1. Fill a bucket (about 1–2 gallons) with water from any available source (e.g., water heater, bottled water, or melted ice).
2. Pour the water quickly and directly into the toilet bowl, aiming toward the drain hole at the bottom. The force of the pouring water will trigger the flush mechanism.

If you need to conserve water:
- Use less water for a partial flush if needed, though this may not clear waste effectively.
- Collect "graywater" from sinks or showers (if available) for flushing.

For clogs without water pressure:
- Add dish soap and hot water to the bowl, wait 20 minutes, then pour in more water to help break up the blockage.

If you have no access to water at all, consider alternative sanitation options until service is restored.

## Question 14: How do I keep warm in my house in winter if the power and heat are out?

**Official guidance:** Keep warm: gather in one room and close it off, layer clothing, blankets, hats; never use an oven, stove, grill or generator for heat (CO); keep CO alarms working.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Power Outages

To keep warm when the power and heat are out, follow these steps immediately:

1.  **Stay in one small room:** Close off unused rooms and stay in a single, interior room (one with no or few exterior walls) if possible. Use towels or blankets to seal gaps under doors to trap heat.
2.  **Insulate your space:** Hang heavy blankets, quilts, or sleeping bags over windows and drafty areas. If you have cardboard, tape it up inside the window frames for extra insulation.
3.  **Bundle up personally:** Wear layers of loose-fitting clothing (wool or fleece is best). Put on a hat, gloves, and warm socks. Use blankets to cover yourself while sitting still; do not lie down unless necessary as body heat dissipates faster when lying flat.
4.  **Use safe heating sources only:** If you have a certified indoor-safe heater (like a kerosene heater with proper ventilation), use it strictly according to instructions and ensure you have working Carbon Monoxide (CO) detectors. **Never** use a gas stove, oven, grill, or camp stove for heat; this releases lethal carbon monoxide. **Never** run a generator indoors, in the garage, or near windows.
5.  **Stay active:** Do light exercises (like marching in place) to generate body heat, but avoid sweating as damp clothes will make you colder.

**Critical Safety Warning:** Carbon monoxide poisoning is a silent killer during outages. If you have any gas-powered device running inside your home, get everyone out and call 911 immediately if you feel dizzy or nauseous.

Sources: Ready.gov (FEMA) - Power Outages

## Question 15: There has been a nuclear explosion nearby. What should I do?

**Official guidance:** Ready.gov: get inside the nearest building, go to the basement or middle, stay inside for 24 hours unless told otherwise, stay tuned to official information; remove outer clothing and wash if exposed.

### Answer A
Sources it read: none

Get inside the nearest sturdy building immediately and stay there. 

1. **Move to a safe spot:** Go to the basement or the middle of the building, staying away from all windows and exterior doors.
2. **Seal the area:** Close and lock all windows and doors. Shut off any vents, fans, heating, or air conditioning that pull in outside air.
3. **Decontaminate if needed:** If you were outside when it happened, take off your outer clothes immediately to remove radioactive dust, then wash your skin with soap and water.
4. **Stay put:** Remain inside for at least 24 hours unless emergency officials tell you otherwise. 
5. **Get updates:** Listen to local radio or official broadcasts for further instructions on when it is safe to leave or if evacuation orders are issued.

Sources: Sheltering from radiation or a chemical release (Ready.gov)

## Question 16: Is canned food still safe to eat after it sat in floodwater?

**Official guidance:** Floodwater: discard home-canned food and damaged commercial cans; undamaged commercial cans can be saved: remove labels, wash, sanitize (boil 2 minutes or bleach solution), relabel.

### Answer A
Sources it read: How to Keep Food and Water Safe After a Disaster or Power Outage

Undamaged commercial canned goods are safe to eat after sitting in floodwater if they are properly cleaned and sanitized. However, **home-canned foods** and any cans that are bulging, rusted through, or open must be thrown away immediately. Floodwater is contaminated and can introduce dangerous bacteria into food containers.

If you have undamaged commercial cans:
1. Remove the paper labels (they harbor dirt and germs).
2. Wash the cans thoroughly with soap and hot water.
3. Sanitize them by soaking or dipping in a solution of 1 cup of unscented chlorine bleach mixed into 5 gallons of clean water.
4. Rinse the cans if desired, then dry them. Relabel them with a marker so you know what they are and their expiration date.

If the cans are damaged (dented deeply at seams, bulging, or leaking), throw them away regardless of whether they were commercially canned or homemade.

Sources: Food safety after a power outage or flood (USDA); How to Keep Food and Water Safe After a Disaster or Power Outage (wikiHow)

## Question 17: Is it safe to take ibuprofen that expired two years ago?

**Official guidance:** Expired ibuprofen: usually safe but may be less effective; the FDA recommends not using expired medicine; ask a pharmacist when possible.

### Answer A
Sources it read: none

**It's generally safe but not guaranteed to be effective.**

Based on research from the FDA's Shelf Life Extension Program and medical sources:

1. **Most medications remain safe** for extended periods after their expiration date, including ibuprofen
2. **Effectiveness may decrease** - two years past expiration means it might not work as well for pain or inflammation relief
3. **Storage matters greatly** - if kept in a cool, dry place away from light and humidity, the medication is more likely to remain stable

**What to check before taking:**
- Look for discoloration, unusual odor, or crumbling tablets
- If it looks/ smells normal, it's probably safe but may be less potent
- Liquid forms or suspensions are more likely to degrade than solid tablets

**Recommendation:** While not dangerous in most cases, the medication is unlikely to provide full pain relief. It's best to replace it with fresh ibuprofen if you have access to a pharmacy. The FDA officially advises against using expired medications because they can't guarantee potency or safety beyond the expiration date.

## Question 18: How do I make oral rehydration solution at home?

**Official guidance:** WHO: 1 liter of clean water + 6 level teaspoons of sugar + half a level teaspoon of salt.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Reduced concentration oral rehydration solution

To make oral rehydration solution at home, use the WHO-recommended recipe:

**Ingredients per litre of safe drinking water:**
- Salt: ½ teaspoon (about 3.5 grams)
- Sugar: 4 teaspoons (about 40 grams)

**Instructions:**
1. Make sure you have one litre of clean, safe drinking water.
2. Add the salt and sugar to the water.
3. Stir thoroughly until both dissolve completely.

**Important notes:**
- Accurate measurements matter—too much salt can be harmful, too little may not work effectively. Use standard measuring spoons if possible.
- This solution is for rehydration due to dehydration from diarrhea or excessive fluid loss.
- Drink in small, frequent sips, even if the person is vomiting.
- The goal is adequate hydration when the person no longer feels thirsty and has normal urine output.

For children: give approximately 200–400 ml per kilogram of body weight over the first 4–6 hours for mild dehydration. For adults, start with about 1–2 litres in the first few hours depending on severity.

## Question 19: Someone working in the heat is confused, very hot and has stopped sweating. What do I do?

**Official guidance:** Heat stroke is an emergency: call 911; move to a cool place; cool fast (cold water immersion, ice packs, wet cloths and fanning); don't give fluids if confused.

### Answer A
Sources it read: none

This is heat stroke, a life-threatening medical emergency. Act immediately:

1. **Call 911 now.** Tell them you suspect heat stroke and the person is confused and not sweating.
2. **Move the person** to a cool, shaded area or air-conditioned space immediately.
3. **Remove extra clothing** to help release body heat.
4. **Cool them down quickly:** Place ice packs on their neck, armpits, and groin. Soak cloths in cold water while fanning them, or immerse them in a cool bath/shower if you can do so safely.
5. **Do not give anything to drink** if they are confused or cannot swallow safely, as this could cause choking.

Keep actively cooling them until emergency medical services arrive. Monitor their breathing and be prepared to perform CPR if they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally.

Sources: Heat stroke (CDC)

## Question 20: How can I tell if home-canned food might have botulism?

**Official guidance:** Possible botulism: bulging, leaking or swollen containers, spurting liquid, foam, cloudy liquid, off smell. Never taste; discard safely (sealed bags), clean spills with bleach; get medical care for symptoms.

### Answer A
Sources it read: USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning: Introduction

You cannot reliably detect botulinum toxin by sight or smell alone—it may be present without any obvious signs. However, here's what to look for:

1. **Bulging or distended lids** – Gas production from bacterial growth can cause the lid to bulge outward.
2. **Leaking jars** – Liquid seeping out of the seal can indicate internal pressure buildup.
3. **Unusual odors** – A foul, sour, or "off" smell when opened (though some contaminated foods may smell normal).
4. **Spurting liquid** – When opening, if liquid spurts out unexpectedly, discard immediately.
5. **Broken seal** – If the lid doesn't stay concave and firmly sealed after cooling, it's compromised.

**Critical safety note:** The USDA warns that even a taste of botulism-contaminated food can be fatal. Since you often cannot see or smell the toxin, always boil low-acid home-canned foods (vegetables, meats, soups) for 10 minutes before eating—add 1 minute per 1,000 ft above sea level. This destroys any heat-labile toxin that might be present.

**When in doubt, throw it out.** Never taste food you suspect may contain botulinum toxin. Symptoms of botulism poisoning (blurred vision, difficulty swallowing, muscle weakness) can appear within 6–48 hours and are life-threatening—seek emergency medical help immediately if symptoms occur after consuming home-canned food.

Sources: USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning; Cooking Q&A

## Question 21: How much water should I store per person for an emergency?

**Official guidance:** Ready.gov: 1 gallon of water per person per day; at least a 3-day supply for evacuation, 2 weeks at home.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Water

Ready.gov (FEMA) recommends storing **at least one gallon of water per person per day** for several days. This covers both drinking and sanitation needs.

Key considerations:
- A normally active adult needs about ¾ gallon of fluids daily, but individual needs vary based on age, health, activity level, diet, and climate.
- Children, nursing mothers, sick people, and those in warm climates may need more water (needs can double in very hot temperatures).
- Store commercially bottled water in its sealed original container in a cool, dark place, or use food-grade storage containers prepared properly.
- Replace non-bottled stored water every six months.

**Sources:** Ready.gov (FEMA) - Water
